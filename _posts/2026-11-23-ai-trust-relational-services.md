How do you feel when you receive an email from someone, and realise it has been written by AI?

This happened to me recently when I complained to a conference that had listed me as a keynote speaker without ever asking me to speak. The message I received back was well-phrased, took responsibility and answered my questions. If a human had written it I might have been mollified. But it was in many ways *too* smooth and included turns of phrase that I have come to recognise as AI-speak. The fact I'd been answered by AI completely undermined my trust in the organisers. What I'd put down to a human oversight became in my mind a sign of an over-reliance on flaky AI.

We don't yet have a norm or requirement for people or organisations to declare when they're using AI to communicate with you. That means we constantly have to guess. A few months ago, I remember booking an eye appointment using WhatsApp. It was only after completing that task that I realised I could have been talking to an AI chatbot, so I asked, out of curiosity, if they were. They were human, or at least that's what they said. In retrospect their slightly robotic and suspiciously rapid responses were probably the result of tools that helped them respond with pre-written messages or formulaic information. I don't think I'd have cared much either way in that case because it was a far more transactional interaction: I just had a task to do, and would have been just as happy filling in a form on a website.

Much of the focus on AI adoption looks at it from the perspective of the person using and in control of AI: the manager summarising their emails; the probation officer taking notes in a meeting; the student drafting their essays. I'm interested in the other side of the equation: the colleague, the prisoner, the lecturer who receives an AI-generated response when they would normally expect to be interacting with a human.

<!--more-->

[Studies in communication psychology show](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44271-025-00387-3) a "AI preference paradox" around receiving AI-generated replies: people rate AI-generated responses as higher in quality, more effective at making them feel heard and more effortful, but generally choose to receive inferior messages from humans instead. This same paradox has been demonstrated for[ patient evaluations of AI-drafted messages](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2851278) from their doctors,[ when they're being consoled](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3491102.3517731), and[ when looking at responses to their own description of a complex situation they're working through](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2319112121).

Of course it's not just about communication – people also don't like decisions being made about them by AI, and it's not just because they might get things wrong (something human decision makers do too). Hence so many reassurances that there will always be a "human in the loop" during algorithmic decision making, even when[ it might not make much difference](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0267364922000292).


## This matters for relational public services

The way people respond to the use of AI by others matters as we try to both make a shift towards more relational public services, and introduce AI into those services. As O'Donnell et al.'s work [Reframing Public Services in Ireland as a Relational Practice](https://publicpolicy.ie/papers/reframing-public-services-in-ireland-as-a-relational-practice/) describes, "Relational Public Services emphasise that good outcomes emerge from trusting, human relationships and continuous learning rather than from rigid targets and standardised transactions, so practitioners work with people as whole persons, adapt support to local contexts, and collaborate across organisational boundaries to address complex social issues".[ Work on relational service design](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/389937645_Relational_Service_Design_and_New_Public_Management_Navigating_a_Paradox) describes how interactions between service users and service providers should be "aimed at creating resonance, openness, trust and connection reflective of the complexity, ambiguity and entanglement of human lives, and are aimed towards creating conditions for people to access their own capability to thrive". [Kinship Works describe those that have a “civic ethic”](https://menders.kinship.works/part-2/civic-ethic/) as seeing “relationships as the work, not an afterthought. Recognising that a deep and authentic human connection is often the way in which change is achieved.”

I am worried that some uses of AI in public services might undermine the [mode shift to relational public services](https://demos.co.uk/research/mode-shifting-whitehall-reform-that-liberates-public-services-to-shift-between-transactional-and-relational-delivery-modes/), and further accelerate the decline in trust in institutions, something[ Antonio Romeo, the Cabinet Secretary, has identified as part of the burning platform requiring changes within the civil service](https://www.gov.uk/government/speeches/rewiring-the-state-and-the-future-civil-service). I felt this acutely listening to[ Paul Sagar on David Runciman's Past Present Future podcast](https://www.ppfideas.com/episodes/can-universities-survive-ai-vs-higher-education) describe the impact of AI in universities, and how suspicious students have become about lecturer use of AI, while lecturers are similarly distrustful about how students are using AI to do their work. The description wasn't just of declining trust, but an escalation in the use of AI on both sides in ways that accelerated distrust.

So my question is: **how will the use of AI in public services affect how people feel about the state?** and more practically, **when and how can we adopt AI within public services to mitigate more negative responses?**


## Why don't people like AI intermediaries?

I don't think we can answer these questions effectively without trying to understand the reasons that people have for responding in the way they do to others' use of AI. I think these fall into three categories:



1. **Responses that are inherent to AI and human psychology.** There are things that we expect in our relationships with other people. We expect people to be able to put themselves in our position and experience empathy and (some degree of) care. We expect them to put an amount of effort into maintaining our relationship with them, similar to the effort we expand. To work closely with others, in the way proposed by relational public services, we need[ high quality connections](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10869-023-09897-5), characterised by "mutual positive regard, trust, and engagement, strengthening a sense of openness to others, competence, and psychological safety". AI intermediaries necessarily break this because the feeling of relationship is not mutual or reciprocal. AI doesn't become connected to us, it can only pretend to be. As[ this study](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12283995/) says, "While AI can convincingly simulate affective tone, its lack of experiential subjectivity and moral agency often leads to diminished perceptions of authenticity and respect once its artificial nature is revealed."
2. **Concerns that might be mitigated** through targeting where and when AI is used, and how it is deployed, talked about and governed. For example:
    * **Concerns we're prepared to trade off.** There is a difference between feeling stuck with AI and choosing to interact with it, and AI intermediaries do have advantages for members of the public such as round-the-clock availability and the accessibility benefits of something that can speak almost every language, be endlessly patient, and tailor its responses to our individual needs. In some situations, people may actually *prefer* to speak to an AI, particularly about sensitive topics where they feel a human might judge them or get defensive. (There are limits to when this is a good idea, though, [particularly when the person is in crisis](https://mentalhealth-uk.org/help-and-information/health-and-wellbeing/artificial-intelligence-and-mental-health/).)
    * **Concerns that could be addressed through attention to process.** Some studies talk about people objecting to AI intermediaries because they can't be held accountable, or because they can't take into account the complexity of human lives. Choosing which stage of the process at which to use AI and emphasising that there will be an opportunity for wider considerations, deeper conversations, and ultimate human accountability later in a process may make people feel better about initial AI interactions.
    * **Concerns that could be addressed through technological safeguards, good governance and procurement.** Some concerns about AI intermediaries arise because of worries about how data is captured and used, particularly by US-based Big Tech companies. Technological safeguards around where data is stored and how it is shared; good governance that properly considers privacy and security; and procurement processes that explicitly build in consideration of the impacts on public trust of using particular technologies or buying from particular firms could help to address these concerns.
3. **Attitudes that could change over time.** The studies I've referenced above discuss that some of the antipathy to AI intermediaries comes about because of a[ general antipathy to AI](https://diffusion.au/uk-ai-compass). Sentiment about AI could change positively or negatively, depending on the other impacts it has on our economy, society and environment. I suspect, though, there will always be a portion of the population who reject AI on moral grounds; public services still need to serve them.

The context in which AI intermediaries are used plainly matters. There's a big difference between "emotionally significant communication", as[ one paper](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12283995/) refers to it and notifications about when your passport is going to be ready. We also have to consider[ individual differences](https://connectedbydata.org/blog/2024/08/18/ai-adoption), which might themselves be situational. Some people will care more, some less, and how you feel on your third call during a time of emotional stress might be different to an initial contact when you're in a good mood. I don't think we should idealise current interactions with government either: we can readily see problems with faceless bureaucrats, stressed professionals, and risk-averse, siloed civil servants who avoid engaging outside their teams. Even with their shortcomings, AI intermediaries could sometimes raise the bar.


## What should we do?

A few years ago,[ Ethan Mollick proposed a "Best Available Human (BAH)" standard](https://www.oneusefulthing.org/p/the-best-available-human-standard): decide whether AI is appropriate by asking whether it would do a better job solving a problem than the best available human that is actually able to help. But "better job" needs to be considered in the round. I would argue that any assessment of AI intermediaries in a public services context should include impacts on service user's feelings of trust in, connection to, and agency in relationships with the state.

Of course, one approach public services could take would be to simply not tell people when they're interacting with an AI. We know AI-generated communication is qualitatively better than what we fallible humans produce, and it's only being told that it's AI that gives these a negative rating. Given all the other benefits of AI, letting AI take over our interface with the state could be perceived as an improvement. And the technology is improving to the extent that people might not be able to tell the difference, even when speaking on the phone or a video call.

I think this would be a mistake. Quite aside from the immorality of misleading citizens in the first place, when people found out – which they surely would – the negative response to the pretence would be even stronger than that resulting from transparent labelling. As in my eye-appointment booking example, every exchange would be called into question, even those that were with humans. That uncertainty and distrust about whether you were talking to an AI would overall result, I predict, in more demand for face-to-face interactions – the only time we can really guarantee we're not conversing with AI – and those are expensive.

So we have to face the questions about whether and when to use AI intermediaries – what our red lines are – with an assumption that any use of AI will be transparent.

One common distinction for thinking about this would be between use of AI in transactional services and in relational services. I'm a bit wary of this, because I think our internal assessment of the public sector is formed of all these many tiny interactions. Below, "transactional" is really a shorthand for services where the majority of interactions have low emotional intensity, and there's a low chance of repeated contact with the same organisation.

In highly transactional public services, then – the classic "getting a passport" example – I think service designers have to do as much as possible to address concerns that are within the scope of that service – things like putting in place technological safeguards, good governance, social-value-oriented procurement, and processes that ensure that AI is never the final arbiter or respondent for meaningful interactions. But then I think we have to give people a choice, while being clear about the trade-offs. Talk to an AI now, or wait 30 minutes before you can speak to a person.

In more relational services – social work, teaching, healthcare and so on – the prevailing narrative is that by automating the routine parts of the job, public servants can be freed up to spend more time on the human parts. The thing I think we have to press on is where this line is drawn. There are some parts of these jobs that have obvious impacts on relationships, like when you're sitting opposite someone in a meeting and talking with them. There are others that are obviously not, like arranging rotas.

And then there are the pieces that are in between. One set are ways of using AI that help professionals to demonstrate care and build relationships beyond what they would do previously. One of my[ favourite examples](https://research.reading.ac.uk/engagement-and-impact/artificial-intelligence-nhs/) of these is AI that helps identify patients who might not attend their hospital appointments and enables staff to "proactively call high-risk patients to understand their individual circumstances and offer tailored support based on specific barriers identified by the model". These kinds of systems can augment relational services, as long as they're not assumed to be completely accurate, and they lead to personal contact from someone who offers support.

On the other side, we have AI being used to carry out activities where members of the public want to be heard, seen and understood by someone else. Marking someone's schoolwork. Updating a patient's record with notes from a consultation. Reading the response to a consultation. These are tasks where the student, patient or citizen isn't in the room physically, but the way they are considered really matters, both to the outcome and to them as a human.

Using AI in these tasks can affect the outcome because they are the places where judgement is exercised and[ leeway](https://www.publicstrategist.com/2015/05/leeway-2/) is granted. They are places a public servant can bring in an understanding of the rich context of a person's life and previous interactions – context that may not be captured in data or known to an AI intermediary. They are places for nuance rather than the flattening and[ stereotyping](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.07850) that AI summaries give us.

But to my central point, I would also argue that how public servants use AI in these places has an impact on how members of the public feel about the state. These kinds of tasks require professionals to be open to others, to put themselves in their shoes, and to consider them as a whole person. They are an essential part of the process of developing a deeper, mutual, relationship. When members of the public see or think an AI intermediary is doing these things instead of a professional human, it's just like having an 'AI' label on a message they receive: they perceive the relationship being developed as a shallow and bureaucratic one. This undermines their sense of[ procedural justice](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0749597818303595). They do not feel heard or seen.

Our trust in and engagement with the state does not just depend on how efficiently and effectively the public sector handles us. It depends on how we feel we are treated. Being palmed off on an AI intermediary can feel disrespectful and insulting, even if all the practical concerns we might have – about data privacy, digital sovereignty, environmental impacts and so on – are addressed. We should be drawing red lines around these liminal spaces where AI is starting to intrude on our relationships, and in particular be very careful about how we introduce it in the public sector in places where people expect to be seen and understood.
